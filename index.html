About

My journey into engineering started with food. Growing up, I didn’t always have access to the quality of food I wanted, and as I got older, I started thinking about what I wanted to change for my younger siblings and, one day, my own children. I found hydroponics while looking for ways to make fresh food more accessible. What started as curiosity eventually became Project OSHN, an idea for a growing system that could give people more control over where and how their food is produced.

OSHN is what brought me back to school. I had an idea I believed in, but every step forward seemed to uncover something I didn’t know yet. I wanted to understand the mechanics of the system, how plants respond to their environment, where the energy comes from, and how materials could change what the system was capable of doing. Eventually, I realized that if I wanted to build solutions to problems I cared about, I needed the education to understand those problems deeply enough to do it well.

I’ve realized that I’m most drawn to problems that don’t already have an obvious answer. I like questioning why something works the way it does, where it falls short, and what might happen if I approach it differently. That curiosity is what pulled me toward research. It gives me the freedom to investigate ideas that are still uncertain, learn from what doesn’t work, and eventually turn what I learn into something useful.

Equitable food access is still deeply personal to me, but my mission has grown beyond food. I want to use engineering and research to create practical solutions that improve how people live. That purpose is what brought me back to school, and it continues to shape what I choose to learn and what I hope to build.

Questions I’m Exploring

How can we rethink the way we grow food?

I’m interested in controlled-environment agriculture, especially systems that make fresh food production more accessible, resource-efficient, and adaptable to different environments.

Can materials become active parts of the systems we design?

I’m curious about materials that can interact with light and energy in useful ways, including their potential to support energy harvesting without compromising the needs of biological systems.

How can we model living systems computationally?

I’m interested in using computational tools to understand plant architecture, environmental response, and the relationships between biological structure and engineered environments.

What everyday problems are waiting for a better design?

Some of my interests begin with much simpler questions: Why does this work this way? Could it work better? I’m interested in mechanical and systems design that turns those questions into practical solutions.
