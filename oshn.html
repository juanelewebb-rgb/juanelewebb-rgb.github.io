Project OSHN

OSHN started because I wanted to make fresh food easier to get. My early designs leaned heavily on mechanical solutions. The more I developed them, the more complicated they got, and each version came with new concerns: water pressure, sanitation, maintenance, and how much energy the whole thing would use.

I started looking for ways to simplify it, and that's what eventually led me to materials. The question I'm working on now is whether the material surrounding the growing system could help generate energy without taking away the light the plants need.

How the research connects

Why I went looking for new materials

I design low-energy growing systems, first for OSHN and then the Hydro-Zinnia Garden for the Dunwoody Engineering Club competition. Along the way I ran into a problem that redrawing the design doesn't fix: microbes growing in the nutrient water.

I tested it for my honors microbiology lab report (PDF). I grew corn and mung bean seedlings in 12 jars of still, non-aerated water, half at room temperature and half at 37°C. The warmer jars had more bacteria.

That told me I couldn't solve this by adjusting the same designs. I needed a more creative approach, and it led me to Donglu Shi's work on materials that handle sunlight selectively.
