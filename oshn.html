Project OSHN

Project OSHN, short for Outer Sourced Hydraulic Nutrition, is a growing system I've been designing, and it's the reason I went back to school. It's a hydroponic setup, where plants grow in nutrient water instead of soil, built from pieces you can add to and move around, so growing food at home is simpler, the plants produce more, and it costs less to build and get going. “Outer sourced” means the system uses its surroundings to generate what it needs. Most controlled-environment farms already use renewable energy, but I want OSHN to be more of a closed loop. I care about it because of where I started. Growing up, I didn't always have the food I wanted, and I kept thinking about what I'd want to be different for my younger siblings and, someday, my own kids.

My first designs leaned hard on mechanical fixes, and every fix brought a new problem: water pressure, sanitation, maintenance, and how much energy the whole thing would use. Energy was the one I couldn't ignore, so I cut the HVAC, the heating, cooling and ventilation, from my controlled-environment design. That brought the power needs down, but it also meant nothing was controlling the temperature inside. Without it, microbial contamination became a high probability. That was a real design flaw in my early iterations. You can see those designs on my research site.

So I tested how much temperature matters. For my honors microbiology lab report (PDF), I grew corn and mung bean seedlings in twelve Kratky jars, a hydroponic method where the plants sit above a still nutrient solution with no pump and no added air. Half were kept at room temperature and half at 37°C. I used Kratky because it's cheap and one of the most common systems home growers use. The warm jars grew more bacteria.

Putting the HVAC back would have undone the low energy use I was after, so I needed a power source that was affordable, modular and adaptable. While I was at Cornell, a friend introduced me to photovoltaics, and I found Donglu Shi's work on Google Scholar. His 2025 review of transparent solar materials is what got me asking whether the material around a growing system could produce energy without blocking the light the plants need.
